Why Cramming Fails: The Case for Spacing Out Study

Most students have crammed for an exam at least once: a long evening of reading the same notes again and again, followed by a test the next morning. It feels productive, and sometimes it even works. Yet for more than a century, psychologists have asked a simple question about timing. If the total amount of study time stays the same, does it matter whether that time is packed into one session or spread across several? The answer, repeated in hundreds of studies, is that spreading study out produces memories that last far longer. Researchers call this the spacing effect.

The first systematic evidence came from the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s. Using himself as the only participant, he memorised lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much practice he needed to relearn them later. He found that repetitions spread over several days required less effort to relearn than the same number of repetitions crowded into a single day. Since then, the pattern has been found with children and adults, in laboratories and classrooms, and with material ranging from foreign vocabulary to simple mathematics procedures.

One large online study shows how the timing matters. More than a thousand adults learned a set of obscure facts, reviewed them once after a gap of between a few minutes and several months, and were then tested up to a year later. The best gap depended on how long the facts needed to be remembered. People tested a week later did best when their review came a day or two after first learning, while people tested after a year benefited most from a gap of several weeks. As a rough guide, the researchers suggested that the gap should be about one-tenth to one-fifth of the time for which the information must be kept.

If spacing works so well, why do so many learners still cram? Part of the answer is that cramming can succeed for a test that comes very soon. Another part lies in how studying feels. When a student rereads a chapter minutes after reading it, the words seem familiar and the ideas flow easily, and learners tend to mistake this ease for learning. In several experiments, students rated massed study as the more effective method even after they had scored higher on a test following spaced study.

Psychologists offer more than one explanation for why spacing helps. One idea is that some forgetting between sessions forces the learner to work harder to bring the information back to mind, and that this effort of recalling it is what strengthens the memory. Another idea is that studying on different days means the material becomes linked to a wider range of situations, which gives the learner more ways to find it again later.

The evidence also has limits. Most experiments have used fairly simple material, such as word pairs or single facts, and many have tested learners within days or weeks. Far fewer studies have examined complex skills, such as writing an argument or solving unfamiliar problems, and in those studies the results are less consistent. Applying the research in schools is not straightforward either. Teachers must cover new topics every week, and time spent returning to old material is time taken from new material. It is also unclear whether laboratory benefits hold when students plan their own revision and must stay motivated without a researcher watching.

Even so, the case for spacing is strong enough to act on. Schools could build review into their timetables, for example by setting homework that returns to topics taught several weeks earlier rather than only the most recent lesson. Individual learners can do the same by planning short reviews instead of one long session the night before. What researchers still need are careful studies in real classrooms, so that schools know how much benefit to expect before they redesign how teaching is organised.
