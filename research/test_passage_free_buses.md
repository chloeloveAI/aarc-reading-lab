Free Buses, Full Seats? What One City's First Year Tells Us

By the Marlow Bay Clean Transport Alliance

The decision

In January last year, Marlow Bay became the first city in the region to make every local bus free to ride. The council's aim was simple: to persuade drivers to leave their cars at home, cut traffic in the city centre, and reduce air pollution. Our Alliance campaigned for the policy for six years, and we believe the first year has proved it right.

What happened to bus use

The numbers are striking. In the twelve months before the change, local buses carried 18.2 million passenger journeys. In the first twelve months of free travel, they carried 24.6 million, an increase of 35 per cent. Buses on the busiest routes, especially those serving the hospital and the university, were often full by the second stop during the morning peak.

Traffic in the centre also fell. Automatic counters on the three main roads into the city recorded an average of 41,000 vehicles a day before the change and 38,500 a day afterwards, a drop of about 6 per cent. Air-quality monitors near the central bus station showed a small improvement in nitrogen dioxide levels over the same period.

Who the new passengers are

To understand where the new journeys came from, the Alliance surveyed 310 passengers at four city-centre stops during one week in October. Of those who said they travelled more by bus than before, 38 per cent said they would previously have driven. Another 41 per cent said they would previously have walked or cycled, and the rest said they would not have made the journey at all.

We see this as a success on every count. People who once drove are now on the bus. People who once stayed at home are now going out, visiting family and using local shops.

What it costs

Fares used to cover about a quarter of the cost of running the bus network. Removing them means the council now pays the full cost, which is an extra 9 million pounds a year, funded mainly by a new charge on workplace parking spaces. Some councillors have argued that this money would have done more good if it had been spent on making buses more frequent and reliable rather than free. They point out that, in surveys before the change, passengers named long waits and missed connections as their biggest complaints, not the price of a ticket.

We understand this argument, but we believe it misses the point. A free bus sends a powerful message that the city is serious about public transport, and that message cannot be bought with timetables alone.

The wider picture

It is worth remembering that last year was unusual. Petrol prices rose by almost a fifth during the spring and stayed high for the rest of the year, and some drivers in every city in the region cut back on car use. Bus journeys in Eastholme, a neighbouring city where fares were not removed, rose by 9 per cent over the same period.

Even so, Marlow Bay's growth was far larger, which shows that free travel, not fuel prices, is the main reason for its success.

What we still do not know

One year is a short time. Some passengers may have tried the bus out of curiosity and will drift back to their cars. The workplace parking charge could be reduced by a future council, and if the money disappears, the policy may not survive. The fall in city-centre traffic may also be partly explained by roadworks on one of the three main roads, which lasted for four months of the year.

The next step

Despite these questions, the evidence from the first year is clear. Free buses work. We call on every city in the region to follow Marlow Bay's example, and we urge the council to make the policy permanent.
