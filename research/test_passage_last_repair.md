**Status:** fictional. All characters and places are invented.

**Why it was written:** AGS must handle stories, not only informational texts. This story has:
- two characters with different motivations
- a clear setting that matters to the plot
- a central conflict
- a change of mind with a stated cause
- a resolution that is not perfect

**It deliberately states no moral.** A reader may infer something about passing on a skill, but the text never says it. So the key-idea map must not force a theme, and AGS must not ask about a "lesson".

The Last Repair

Mr Lo had mended watches at the same stall in the Kam Wah covered market for forty-one years. On Monday a notice appeared on every pillar: the market would close on Friday, and the building would be pulled down to make room for a car park. By Tuesday most of his drawers were already empty, and his tools were wrapped in newspaper inside a biscuit tin.

That afternoon a young courier stopped at his counter. Her name was Mei, and she held out a small gold watch with a cracked strap. It had belonged to her grandmother, she explained, and it had not ticked since the funeral. Her sister was getting married on Saturday, and Mei wanted her to wear it.

Mr Lo shook his head without opening the case. He did not take work he could not finish, he said, and by Friday there would be no stall to finish it at.

Mei did not argue. But the next morning she was back, carrying parcels for the noodle shop opposite, and she stood for a while watching him pack. On Wednesday she came again. This time she pointed at a tray of tiny wheels and asked whether the one with the missing tooth was the escape wheel. Mr Lo looked up properly for the first time.

"Who taught you that?" he asked.

"Nobody," she said. "I looked it up on the bus. Twice."

He laughed, and then he was quiet for a long moment. He had learned the same way, he told her, standing behind a counter that no longer existed, watching an old man who would not let him touch anything for a year.

On Thursday he unwrapped the biscuit tin. He did not repair the watch. Instead he sat Mei on his own stool, put the loupe to her eye, and guided her hands while she lifted out the broken wheel and fitted the one he had kept in a matchbox for twenty years. Twice she dropped a screw, and twice they searched the floor together, on their knees between the empty stalls.

By evening the watch was ticking. It lost about two minutes a day, and Mr Lo said he could not fix that without proper tools and another week.

On Friday the stall was bare. Mei came to say goodbye before her last delivery. Mr Lo pressed the loupe into her hand and would not take it back.

"Two minutes slow," he said, "is close enough for a wedding."

She fastened the watch to her own wrist for safekeeping and rode off into the traffic. Behind her, the workmen were already taking down the market sign, one letter at a time.
