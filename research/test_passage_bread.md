Why Bread Rises

A lump of flour and water baked in an oven becomes a flat, hard cracker. Add a pinch of yeast and give it time, and the same ingredients become a light loaf full of holes. The difference comes from three things working together: living yeast, a stretchy network of protein, and heat.

Yeast makes the gas

Yeast is a tiny single-celled fungus. When it is mixed into dough, it begins to feed on sugars. Flour contains only a little free sugar, but it also contains starch, and natural enzymes in the flour slowly break some of that starch down into sugars the yeast can use. As the yeast feeds, it gives off carbon dioxide gas and a small amount of alcohol. This process is called fermentation.

On its own, this gas would simply escape. Something has to hold it in.

Gluten traps the gas

That something is gluten. Wheat flour contains two proteins, glutenin and gliadin. When water is added, they link together to form gluten, a network that is both stretchy and strong. Kneading the dough lines up these protein strands and makes the network tighter and more elastic, rather like turning a tangle of threads into a fine net.

As the yeast releases carbon dioxide, the gas collects in thousands of tiny pockets inside this net. The gluten stretches around each pocket like the skin of a balloon, so the dough swells instead of letting the gas leak away. This is why bread made from flours with little gluten, such as rice flour, does not rise in the same way without extra help.

Temperature controls the speed

Yeast is sensitive to temperature. In a cool kitchen, fermentation is slow, and dough may take many hours to double in size. In a warm place, at around body temperature, the yeast works much faster. If the dough becomes too hot, however, the yeast dies, and no more gas is made. Bakers therefore choose the temperature of the water and the room to control how quickly the dough rises.

Salt also changes the pace. It slows the yeast down slightly and tightens the gluten, which is why most recipes add only a small, measured amount.

Heat finishes the job

When the dough goes into the oven, it rises sharply one last time. The heat makes the trapped gas expand, the alcohol turns to vapour, and water in the dough turns to steam. All of this pushes the gluten pockets wider. Bakers call this final burst "oven spring".

Soon afterwards, the heat kills the yeast. The starch absorbs water and sets, and the gluten firms up, fixing the holes in place. The outside of the loaf dries and browns into a crust, which also gives bread much of its flavour.

So a well-risen loaf depends on a sequence. The yeast must produce enough gas, the gluten must be strong enough to hold it, and the heat must expand the gas before setting the structure around it. If any one step fails, the bread is likely to be dense and heavy.
